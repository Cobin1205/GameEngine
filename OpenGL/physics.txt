The most common serious 3D hobby-engine physics architecture is:

Rigid body dynamics + broadphase BVH/DBVT + GJK/EPA narrowphase + sequential impulse solver

This is the same family used by Bullet, PhysX, Jolt, ReactPhysics3D, and most custom hobby engines.

Since your objects are already defined by vertices, this is actually ideal for convex mesh rigid bodies.

The most common 3D hobby-engine stack
1) Rigid body simulation (6 DOF)

In 3D every body needs:

position
orientation quaternion
linear velocity
angular velocity
inverse mass
inverse inertia tensor
force accumulator
torque accumulator

This is the standard 6-degree-of-freedom rigid body setup:

3 positional axes
3 rotational axes

This is the backbone of almost every hobby 3D engine.

2) Broad phase: DBVT / BVH

For 3D, the most common broadphase in hobby engines is:

Dynamic Bounding Volume Tree (DBVT)

This is usually better than octrees for moving rigid bodies.

Great options:

dynamic AABB tree
BVH
sweep and prune (good for many boxes)
spatial hash (good for lots of tiny dynamic objects)

Most hobby engines choose DBVT first.

3) Narrow phase: GJK + EPA

This is the big one.

For 3D convex meshes made from vertices, the most common approach is:

GJK for overlap

EPA for penetration depth and normal

This is far more common in 3D than SAT for arbitrary convex hulls.

Why this is ideal for your vertex objects

If every object is a vertex mesh:

compute convex hull once
use support mapping
run GJK
use EPA to get MTV / normal

That’s basically how many real engines do it.

SAT is still fine for:

OBBs
boxes
specific polyhedra

But GJK/EPA scales better for general convex meshes.

4) Solver: Sequential impulses

This is still the most common answer.

Even in 3D, hobby engines overwhelmingly use:

Sequential Impulse constraint solving

This handles:

collision response
friction
stacking
joints
resting contacts
ragdolls later

It’s the “industry standard hobbyist solver.”

My honest recommendation for your engine

Because your engine is 3D and vertex-based, the most common and best hobby route is:

Convex hull colliders + GJK/EPA + sequential impulses

That is the standard “I’m building a real 3D engine” route.

If you try to jump straight into concave mesh-vs-mesh physics, it gets painful fast.

The winning strategy is:

render mesh != physics mesh

Use:

visual mesh = arbitrary vertices
physics mesh = convex hull or compound hulls

That’s what most real engines do.

My strongest advice

Your first milestone should be:

stable cube stacking

If your engine can stack:

20 cubes
no jitter
no sinking
no exploding rotation

you’re on the right path.

That test exposes nearly every major solver flaw.
